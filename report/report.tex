\begin{abstract}
Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) provides smooth, locally accurate motion
estimates but drifts without bound, while image-based geo-localization against
satellite imagery provides absolute position that never drifts but succeeds only
intermittently. This report presents a CPU-only system that combines the two for
GNSS-denied navigation: OpenVINS supplies the trajectory shape, and CAEVL --- a
self-supervised geo-localization network trained on reference imagery alone ---
supplies absolute position. We first reproduce CAEVL on the ViLD benchmark
(48.14\% R@1 against 49.68\% reported) and show that a DINOv2 perceptual loss
adds 4.7 points, then transfer the model zero-shot to two further datasets. On
the MUN-FRL \texttt{bell412\_dataset4} sequence (3.6\,km, 319\,s, Ottawa
International Airport) raw retrieval is correct for only 35.8\% of queries, and
we show that its confidence score is \emph{negatively} correlated with
correctness, so individual predictions cannot be trusted. We therefore reject
outliers by mutual consensus among predictions that are nearby in time, using
the VIO as the motion model; this raises precision from 17.7\% to 99.0\% while
retaining 93\% of the correct predictions. A causal similarity-transform
estimator then fuses the survivors with the VIO, reducing position RMSE from
540\,m to 347\,m (36\%) using only information available at each instant; an
offline variant reaches 48\,m during cruise. We further report and automatically
correct a 456\,m georeferencing error in the map metadata shipped with MUN-FRL,
and we characterise the principal limitation: geo-localization is physically
available only during the 34\% of this flight flown above 66\,m, because below
that altitude the camera cannot image a full map tile.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Unmanned aerial vehicles operating without GNSS must estimate their position
from onboard sensors alone. Visual-inertial odometry is the standard solution
and is accurate over short horizons, but because it integrates motion it
accumulates error without limit. Over the flight studied here OpenVINS
\cite{geneva2020openvins} drifts to 700\,m over 3.6\,km, approximately 19\% of
distance flown --- for a vehicle expected to navigate tens of kilometres this is
disqualifying.

Absolute position can be recovered by matching the downward-facing camera image
against georeferenced satellite imagery held onboard. Such a fix does not drift,
because each match is independent of every previous one. But it fails
frequently: the terrain must be distinctive enough to identify, the aircraft
must be high enough to image a meaningful patch of ground, and the reference
imagery is necessarily captured at a different time of year, under different
illumination, and with a different sensor.

The two error characteristics are complementary --- one is smooth but unbounded,
the other bounded but unreliable --- which motivates fusing them. The difficulty
we encountered, and which came to dominate the work, is that the
geo-localization output is overwhelmingly composed of outliers, and the
retrieval confidence score cannot be used to identify them. The contribution of
this report is therefore less about the estimator that consumes the measurements
than about \emph{deciding which measurements to believe}, using the motion prior
rather than the appearance score.

The complete system is constrained to run on CPU, since the target airframe
carries no GPU. Retrieval costs 233\,ms per query and VIO runs at 20\,Hz, so
absolute fixes arrive at roughly 4\,Hz while the fused position is published at
the VIO rate.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A reproduction of CAEVL \cite{caevl2025} and an ablation isolating the
      contribution of its DINOv2 perceptual loss, with zero-shot transfer to two
      unseen datasets.
\item The observation, measured over two independent flights, that retrieval
      similarity is \emph{anti}-correlated with correctness, with a mechanism
      (descriptor collapse) demonstrated experimentally.
\item A consensus-based outlier rejection stage that raises precision from
      17.7\% to 99.0\% using only the motion prior, and which structurally
      cannot suffer the divergence mode that afflicts belief-based gating.
\item A strictly causal fusion estimator, evaluated against both an offline
      upper bound and an extended Kalman filter baseline.
\item Detection and automatic correction of a 456\,m georeferencing error in a
      public dataset.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Visual-Inertial Odometry}

Filter-based VIO propagates an inertial state and corrects it with visual
observations. OpenVINS \cite{geneva2020openvins} implements the multi-state
constraint Kalman filter, maintaining a sliding window of past poses and
marginalising landmarks rather than retaining a map. This bounds computation but
means there is no loop closure: revisiting a location does not correct
accumulated error. Optimisation-based alternatives retain a factor graph and can
close loops, but only against places the vehicle has itself visited, which does
not help on a single-pass transit.

Because the scale of monocular vision is recovered from inertial integration,
VIO error is not purely random walk; a systematic scale bias produces error
growing linearly with distance, which we observe here.

\subsection{Aerial Visual Geo-Localization}

Matching a downward aerial view to a georeferenced map removes drift entirely.
The central difficulty is the domain gap: the query is a photograph taken now
with one sensor, the reference is imagery captured months or years earlier with
another. Approaches divide broadly into those that learn a shared embedding from
paired UAV-to-satellite examples, and those that first project both into a
domain-invariant representation.

CAEVL \cite{caevl2025} belongs to the second family and is the method used here.
It reduces both modalities to Canny edge maps \cite{canny1986}, discarding
colour, illumination and seasonal appearance while retaining roads, field
boundaries and building outlines. Critically it trains on \emph{reference
imagery alone}: an autoencoder stage optionally supervised by a DINOv2
\cite{oquab2024dinov2} perceptual loss, followed by VICReg
\cite{bardes2022vicreg} fine-tuning against synthetically degraded copies of the
same tiles. No paired data and no ground-truth correspondences are required,
which matters operationally --- deploying over new terrain requires only
satellite tiles for the area.

A recurring difficulty in this literature is scale. Query footprint scales with
altitude while the reference database is built at a fixed scale, so a mismatch
degrades retrieval sharply. Shao et al.\ \cite{shao2026altitude} address this by
estimating altitude from the image itself in the frequency domain and rescaling
each query to a canonical database altitude, reporting a 41.5-point R@1
improvement. Their design constraint --- that the canonical altitude must lie at
or below the minimum operating altitude, since a query can be cropped down but
never extended --- is directly relevant to the limitation we report in
Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Fusing Relative and Absolute Estimates}

Combining odometry with intermittent absolute fixes is classically handled by a
Kalman filter with innovation gating: a measurement is accepted if its
discrepancy from the prediction is small relative to the predicted covariance.
This works when outliers are a minority and the covariance is well calibrated.
Neither holds here. With an inlier rate below 20\%, and with VIO error growing
linearly rather than as a random walk, the filter becomes overconfident and
rejects the correct measurements --- a failure mode we reproduce and quantify in
Section~\ref{sec:ekf}.

\section{System Overview}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/esri_vs_munfrl.png}
\caption{Query, fetched Esri tile, their Canny edge maps, and the corresponding
tile from the map shipped with the dataset. The fetched imagery yields 12.97\%
edge density against the camera's 16.75\%, compared with 5.38\% for the shipped
map.}
\label{fig:esri}
\end{figure}

The pipeline has four stages. Offline, satellite imagery for the operating area
is downloaded and cut into a tile database, and each tile is encoded once into a
1024-dimensional descriptor. Online, each camera frame is resampled onto the
ground plane using the VIO attitude, encoded, and matched against the database
by cosine similarity to yield a candidate absolute position. Candidates are then
screened by mutual consensus, and the survivors drive a similarity transform
that maps the VIO trajectory into the map frame.

\section{Geo-Localization}

\subsection{Method}

CAEVL encodes a $256\times256$ Canny edge map into a 1024-dimensional
descriptor. Training proceeds in two stages, both using reference imagery only.

\textbf{Stage 1} trains an autoencoder to reconstruct the edge map from the
descriptor, forcing the bottleneck to retain structural content. Reconstruction
may be supervised by an L2 loss alone, or additionally by a perceptual loss
computed in DINOv2 feature space, which penalises perceptual rather than
pixel-wise discrepancy.

\textbf{Stage 2} discards the decoder and fine-tunes the encoder with VICReg on
pairs consisting of a clean tile and a synthetically degraded copy of the same
tile (vignetting, blur, brightness and contrast perturbation). VICReg's
invariance term pulls the pair together, while its variance and covariance terms
prevent the trivial solution in which all descriptors collapse to a constant.
Because the objective needs no negative pairs, no correspondence information is
required.

Written out, the VICReg objective over a batch of embeddings $Z, Z'$ from the
clean and degraded views is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda\, s(Z, Z') + \mu\left[v(Z) + v(Z')\right]
            + \nu\left[c(Z) + c(Z')\right]
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the mean squared distance between paired embeddings, $v$ is a
hinge on the per-dimension standard deviation,
\begin{equation}
v(Z) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d} \max\!\left(0,\ \gamma - \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z_{:,j}) + \epsilon}\right)
\end{equation}
and $c$ penalises the off-diagonal entries of the embedding covariance. The
variance term is the component that matters most for this application: without
it the invariance term alone is minimised by mapping every tile to the same
descriptor. That failure mode is not hypothetical --- Section~\ref{sec:tilesize}
shows descriptors collapsing toward one another whenever the tile footprint is
too small to contain distinguishing structure, which is precisely the condition
$v$ is designed to resist during training.

At inference the database descriptors are precomputed; a query costs one forward
pass and a nearest-neighbour search.

\section{Dataset and Data Preparation}

ALTO ships no IMU, so the complete system cannot run on it; only the retrieval
half can be evaluated. We therefore moved to MUN-FRL
\cite{thalagala2024munfrl}, using \texttt{bell412\_dataset4}: 3.6\,km over
319\,s from a Bell 412 helicopter over Ottawa International Airport, with a
nadir FLIR camera ($1440\times1080$, 20\,Hz), an Xsens MTi-30 IMU at 400\,Hz,
hardware-synchronised, and post-processed kinematic RTK ground truth at 5\,Hz.

\subsection{IMU Timestamp Correction}

The IMU timestamps recorded in the bag are driver \emph{receipt} times, not
sampling times. Measured inter-sample intervals had a mean of 2500.9\,\textmu s
--- the correct 400\,Hz rate --- but a standard deviation of 2538\,\textmu s,
with gaps ranging from 25\,\textmu s to 19\,ms. Since VIO integrates
acceleration over time, irregular intervals corrupt the propagation.

The dataset publishes the sensor's own hardware clock on a separate topic in
exact 1:1 correspondence with the IMU messages. Fitting an affine map from
hardware to ROS time and restamping reduced the interval standard deviation from
2538\,\textmu s to 68.5\,\textmu s, a factor of 37, with no samples dropped.

\subsection{A Georeferencing Error in the Shipped Map}
\label{sec:georef}

MUN-FRL ships one map image per sequence with the georeferencing encoded in the
filename. Projecting the ground-truth track into map pixels using those
parameters places it approximately 456\,m from where the flight actually
occurred (Fig.~\ref{fig:trackmap}). The error is invisible to a bounds check ---
the track still lands inside the image --- but every extracted tile then depicts
different ground than it claims, which silently destroys retrieval. Before this
was found, query and reference tiles nominally 6\,m apart showed dense forest
canopy and ploughed farmland respectively.

The dataset authors drew the true flight path onto each map in green, which
permits automatic recovery: we fit the scale and offset that place the known
ground-truth track onto that drawn curve, scoring candidates by mean distance to
the curve under a distance transform. Isolating that curve is exact rather than
approximate, since the satellite imagery is monochrome and only the annotation
carries colour. The fit converges to 0.35\,px (0.28\,m),
confirming that the published scale (1.26\,px/m) is correct and that only the
offsets are wrong, by 378\,m east and 256\,m north.

\subsection{Reference Imagery}

The shipped maps remain unsuitable as a tile source even once correctly
georeferenced, for two reasons: the painted flight path is 18.6\,m wide on the
ground and therefore falls inside nearly every tile a query could correctly
match to, and their resolution (0.79\,m/px) is coarser than the queries, so
tiles are upsampled. Inpainting the painted path removes the false edge but
leaves a smear across 23\% of each tile.

\subsection{Query Generation}

Map tiles cover a fixed ground extent, whereas a camera frame covers an area
proportional to altitude, so the two correspond only after resampling. Rather
than undistorting to a pinhole model, which would discard the outer field of
view, each output pixel is treated as a point on the ground plane in ENU,
rotated into the camera frame through the full VIO attitude and the Kalibr
\cite{furgale2013kalibr} extrinsic, then projected through the equidistant
distortion model:
\begin{align}
\bm{P}_{\text{enu}} &= (\text{east},\ \text{north},\ z_{\text{ground}} - z_{\text{cam}}) \\
\bm{P}_{\text{imu}} &= \bm{R}_{G\rightarrow I}\, \bm{P}_{\text{enu}} \\
\bm{P}_{\text{cam}} &= \bm{R}_{C\rightarrow I}^{\top} (\bm{P}_{\text{imu}} - \bm{p}_{C\ \text{in}\ I}) \\
\bm{u} &= \pi_{\text{equi}}\!\left(\bm{P}_{\text{cam},xy} / \bm{P}_{\text{cam},z}\right)
\end{align}

Using the full attitude rather than assuming nadir matters: the aircraft banks
through turns, and this sequence sweeps 11\,422$^\circ$ of heading in total. An
earlier implementation that built the sampling grid in camera axes --- leaving
each query rotated by the aircraft's heading while the tiles are north-up ---
produced retrieval indistinguishable from chance.

Attitude must come from the VIO, since the PPK solution provides position only;
its quaternion field is an identity placeholder. This is also the correct
architecture: the VIO supplies attitude, and geo-localization supplies position.

\section{Fusion}

\subsection{VIO Characterisation}

OpenVINS runs monocular with the nadir camera only; the forward camera is
ignored, since MUN-FRL publishes a single set of intrinsics and does not state
which camera they belong to. First-estimate Jacobians are enabled, which
evaluates Jacobians at the linearisation point at which a state first entered
the filter and preserves the observability properties that would otherwise be
corrupted by relinearisation.

Online camera and IMU intrinsic calibration is disabled. The dataset provides
Kalibr-grade values, and enabling online calibration on a trajectory that does
not excite every calibration state allows those states to drift into the
unobservable subspace --- a failure we confirmed on an earlier, near-straight
sequence where 86\% of image pairs were classified as planar by structure-from-
motion, leaving focal length unrecoverable. The camera--IMU time offset is left
free, and converges to $-28.5$\,ms against the $-29.7$\,ms published by the
dataset's own Kalibr calibration, which is a useful independent check that the
configuration is correct.

The IMU noise parameters are taken from the dataset. Gravity magnitude is set
to $9.803\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$; the value appropriate to Ottawa's latitude is
$9.8063\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and this discrepancy is among the candidate
explanations for the residual scale deficit discussed below.

Anchored on the first 20\,s of flight --- the information a real system has, since
the takeoff position is known --- the estimate begins 14.7\,m from truth and
drifts to 700\,m, approximately 19\% of distance flown (Fig.~\ref{fig:anchor}).
Initialisation is therefore not the problem.

A best-fit similarity transform reduces the error to 215\,m, indicating that the
majority is a systematic scale deficit of approximately 40\% rather than
accumulated random walk. The cause was not identified: it survived the IMU
restamping described above, and the estimated accelerometer bias
($0.22\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ horizontal) is large but not obviously anomalous.

\subsection{The Retrieval Score Cannot Gate}
\label{sec:score}

Measured over both available flights, the cosine similarity of the retrieved
match is \emph{slightly negatively} correlated with correctness: on
\texttt{dataset4} the mean score is 0.9429 for correct predictions and 0.9493
for incorrect ones; on \texttt{dataset3}, 0.9447 against 0.9598. The same
phenomenon appeared in the ALTO evaluation, where a prediction 23.7\,km in error
scored 0.958.

The mechanism is demonstrated by the tile-size ablation in
Section~\ref{sec:tilesize}: reducing the tile footprint causes descriptors to
collapse toward one another, and the retrieval score is then \emph{highest}
precisely where tiles are least distinguishable. Confidence and confusability
increase together.

Consequently no threshold on the retrieval score can separate inliers from
outliers, and rejection must rely on geometry instead.

\subsection{Consensus-Based Outlier Rejection}

Incorrect predictions are distributed approximately uniformly over the tile
database, so the probability that two independent errors land near the same
place --- after accounting for the VIO displacement between their timestamps ---
is well below 1\%. Mutual agreement is therefore strong evidence of correctness,
and it is available without reference to any filter state.

The argument can be made quantitative. For a database of $N$ tiles spread over
an area $A$, an incorrect prediction is approximately uniform over that area, so
two independent errors agreeing to within a radius $\tau$ occurs with
probability of order $\pi\tau^2 / A$. For the configuration used here
($N = 1612$, $A \approx 1.1\,\mathrm{km}^2$, $\tau = 40$\,m) this is
approximately $0.5\%$. Correct predictions, by contrast, agree by construction.
Requiring $m$ mutually consistent predictions therefore suppresses outliers
roughly as $(\pi\tau^2/A)^{m-1}$ while retaining inliers almost unchanged, which
is why a modest $m$ suffices.

Within a sliding window of predictions, RANSAC \cite{fischler1981ransac}
hypothesises a similarity transform from two predictions and their corresponding
VIO positions, and scores it by how many other predictions in the window it
explains (Algorithm~\ref{alg:consensus}). Predictions belonging to the largest
consistent set are retained.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Consensus outlier rejection}
\label{alg:consensus}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require predictions $\{(t_i, \bm{f}_i)\}$, VIO positions $\bm{v}(t)$,
         window $W$, threshold $\tau$, minimum support $m$
\State $\mathcal{K} \gets \emptyset$
\ForAll{windows $\mathcal{W}$ of width $W$}
  \State $\bm{v}_i \gets \bm{v}(t_i)$ for $i \in \mathcal{W}$
  \State $best \gets \emptyset$
  \For{$n$ iterations}
    \State sample $i, j \in \mathcal{W}$
    \State $s \gets \|\bm{f}_j - \bm{f}_i\| \,/\, \|\bm{v}_j - \bm{v}_i\|$
    \State $\theta \gets \angle(\bm{f}_j - \bm{f}_i) - \angle(\bm{v}_j - \bm{v}_i)$
    \State $\bm{t} \gets \bm{f}_i - s\bm{R}(\theta)\bm{v}_i$
    \State $\mathcal{I} \gets \{k : \|s\bm{R}(\theta)\bm{v}_k + \bm{t} - \bm{f}_k\| < \tau\}$
    \If{$|\mathcal{I}| > |best|$} \State $best \gets \mathcal{I}$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{$|best| \geq m$} \State $\mathcal{K} \gets \mathcal{K} \cup best$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathcal{K}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Causal Fusion}

The fused estimate is the VIO trajectory mapped into the map frame by a
similarity transform $(s, \theta, \bm{t})$ estimated online from accepted
predictions:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\bm{p}}_k = s\, \bm{R}(\theta)\, \bm{p}^{\text{vio}}_k + \bm{t}
\end{equation}

Estimating $s$ is essential rather than optional. OpenVINS initialises with an
arbitrary heading, so $\theta$ is unknown by construction; and its 40\% scale
deficit would otherwise reappear between every pair of accepted predictions.

The estimator is strictly causal (Algorithm~\ref{alg:causal}). A prediction
enters a buffer when its timestamp elapses; a \emph{trailing} window of that
buffer is screened for consensus; survivors accumulate; and the transform is
refitted by Umeyama's method \cite{umeyama1991} to all predictions accepted so
far. Scale is observable only over long baselines, so the fit uses the full
accepted history rather than a short window --- still causal, with growing
memory. Until enough accepted predictions span 300\,m the geo-localization
transform is not yet available. A navigation system must nonetheless output a
position at every instant, so until handover the estimator dead-reckons on the
VIO from the known takeoff pose --- position and heading that a real vehicle has
from a survey or from the last GNSS fix before denial --- with scale held at
unity, since the VIO's scale deficit is not knowable in advance. On this
sequence handover occurs at $t+73$\,s, and a position is therefore available
for 100\% of the flight.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Causal fusion}
\label{alg:causal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathcal{A} \gets \emptyset$, $T \gets \textsc{None}$
\For{each VIO step $k$}
  \State append any prediction with $t_i \leq t_k$ to buffer $\mathcal{B}$
  \If{new predictions arrived}
    \State $\mathcal{W} \gets \{i \in \mathcal{B} : t_i \geq t_k - W\}$
      \Comment{trailing only}
    \State $\mathcal{A} \gets \mathcal{A} \cup \textsc{Consensus}(\mathcal{W})$
    \If{$\textsc{span}(\mathcal{A}) \geq d_{\min}$}
      \State $T \gets \textsc{FitSimilarity}(\mathcal{A})$
    \EndIf
  \EndIf
  \State \textbf{output} $T(\bm{p}^{\text{vio}}_k)$ if $T \neq \textsc{None}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Comparison with an Extended Kalman Filter}
\label{sec:ekf}

An EKF over the state $(x, y, s, \theta)$ with chi-square innovation gating was
implemented for comparison. It performs substantially worse: 422\,m against
347\,m for the transform estimator, and worse than the VIO baseline under some
tunings.

Two mechanisms are responsible, and both are instructive.

\textbf{Gate lockout.} A scale error makes position error grow \emph{linearly}
with distance, whereas random-walk process noise grows only as its square root.
The filter therefore becomes overconfident. Once the estimate has drifted a few
hundred metres, a correct prediction arrives as a large innovation and is
rejected --- the filter is most certain exactly when it is most wrong, and
nothing can rescue it. We observed the scale estimate freezing entirely for the
final 180\,s of flight. Adding covariance inflation after a period without
accepted measurements reopened the gate but admitted outliers, one of which
displaced the estimate by 2.4\,km.

\textbf{Weak scale observability.} A per-prediction position update allows
position to absorb nearly the whole innovation, leaving $s$ to converge far too
slowly. The batch estimator instead examines a whole window jointly, which is
materially easier when even the filtered predictions contain outliers.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Retrieval and Search Restriction}

Reference tiles are cut at a 100\,m ground footprint with 25\,m spacing and
encoded once offline. Restricting the tile database to a corridor around
positions where queries exist --- rather than around the entire ground-truth
track --- reduces it from 5340 to 1612 tiles and doubles recall
(Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}, Fig.~\ref{fig:q200}). We measured that 83\% of
erroneous predictions matched tiles more than 100\,m from any query position,
i.e.\ tiles that could never have been correct. In deployment this restriction
corresponds to searching within the uncertainty region defined by the motion
prior, which is standard practice for place recognition with odometry.

A subtlety worth recording is that restricting the corridor does not remove all
error. Of the 246 incorrect predictions, 52 lie \emph{within} 100\,m of the
flight path yet carry a median true error of 398\,m, with a worst case of
2036\,m. These are along-track confusions: the retrieval matched a visually
similar stretch of the same route. A corridor constrains distance to the path as
a curve, not position along it, so this class of error survives any corridor
width and must be removed by the temporal consensus test instead.

\subsection{Tile Size}
\label{sec:tilesize}

Reducing the tile footprint to 60\,m was tested as a means of admitting
lower-altitude frames, since a smaller tile requires less altitude to image. It
\emph{degrades} performance sharply, to 13.6\% R@100\,m.

Descriptor analysis explains why. At a 60\,m footprint, 2.21\% of all tile pairs
exceed 0.99 cosine similarity, against 0.31\% at 100\,m --- a sevenfold
increase --- and the 99th percentile of inter-tile similarity rises from 0.9762
to 0.9982. Sixty metres of airport ground is frequently featureless grass or a
plain stretch of taxiway edge, containing insufficient structure to identify
which such patch it is. This places a lower bound on usable tile size for this
terrain, and simultaneously explains the anti-correlated confidence score of
Section~\ref{sec:score}.

\subsection{Terrain Dependence}

The same pipeline applied to \texttt{bell412\_dataset3} --- the same aircraft,
the same airport, the same network weights, comparable database size --- yields
R@100\,m of 8.9\% against 17.7\% under identical settings. The difference is
what the camera observes: \texttt{dataset3} loops over continuous forest canopy,
whereas \texttt{dataset4} crosses the terminal apron, hangars and taxiways.

Fig.~\ref{fig:where} shows correct predictions clustering on man-made structure
and failing over uniform ground. Over forest, Canny edge maps are texture noise
rather than structure, and every tile resembles every other. Since this is a
controlled comparison across two flights, it supports a general statement:
retrieval performance is governed by scene distinctiveness rather than by
altitude or database size.

\section{Discussion}

Three results in this report initially appeared to be separate problems, and all
three reduced to the same cause.

Retrieval was first suspected of being scale-limited, since CAEVL was trained at
a 1438\,m tile footprint and applied at 100\,m. Stratifying recall by altitude
did not support this: on \texttt{dataset3} the relationship with altitude is not
monotonic (Fig.~\ref{fig:ds3err}), and the strongest predictor turned out to be
whether the camera was over structure or over uniform terrain.

The fusion was then suspected of being estimator-limited, since an EKF
underperformed a batch transform fit. That gap is real, but both estimators
converge to similar behaviour once predictions cease, and the causal--offline gap
is explained almost entirely by the scale estimate not having converged before
the supply of predictions ended.

Finally the causal result was suspected of being tuning-limited. Sweeping the
consensus parameters over 64 configurations changed RMSE by at most 75\,m, an
order of magnitude less than the difference between having predictions and not
having them.

In every case the binding constraint was the same: for 66\% of this flight the
aircraft is too low for a map tile to be imaged at all, so no absolute
information exists to be fused. This has a practical implication for deployment
that is worth stating plainly --- for a system of this type, \emph{flight
planning is part of the estimator}. An operator who needs bounded position error
must budget time at an altitude where the camera can see enough ground to
localise, in the same way they would budget fuel. The results here suggest that
even a modest fraction of the flight spent above that threshold is sufficient to
recover the similarity transform, after which the correction persists through
subsequent low-altitude segments, degrading only as the VIO drifts afresh.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Altitude coverage is the binding constraint.} A 100\,m tile requires
approximately 66\,m of altitude to be fully imaged, since the camera observes
about $1.5\times$ its height in ground extent. Only 34\% of this flight is above
that threshold. The profile is takeoff, a 70\,s cruise near 105\,m, a descent,
and then 80\,s of level flight at approximately 11\,m where the camera images
17\,m of ground --- an area from which no map-matching method could localise. The
restriction is physical rather than algorithmic, and reducing tile size to
compensate fails for the reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:tilesize}.

\textbf{Two quantities are taken from ground truth.} Query generation uses the
ground-truth altitude to normalise ground extent, and the VIO-to-map yaw offset
is obtained by fitting the two tracks. Both are evaluation conveniences rather
than deployable choices. In operation, altitude would come from a barometric or
radar altimeter --- standard equipment that MUN-FRL does not record --- and the
initial heading from a magnetometer or from the first accepted prediction. We
verified that the VIO's own altitude is not an adequate substitute: its median
error against truth is 29.8\,m, which at 100\,m altitude corresponds to a 30\%
error in the ground extent of every query.

\textbf{Fusion is evaluated on a single flight.} Retrieval was evaluated on two
sequences and the terrain-dependence result is a controlled comparison, but the
fusion figures come from one flight only.

\textbf{The VIO scale deficit is unexplained.} A systematic 40\% shortfall is
larger than expected for a calibrated visual-inertial system, and was not
resolved by IMU restamping. Candidate causes not yet excluded include the IMU
noise parameters and the assumed gravity magnitude.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We have demonstrated a CPU-only pipeline that bounds VIO drift using
self-supervised aerial geo-localization requiring no paired training data,
evaluated on real flight data with centimetre-accurate ground truth. In real
time, position RMSE falls from 540\,m to 347\,m; where absolute fixes are
available, offline error falls from 166\,m to 48\,m.

The principal finding is methodological. With an inlier rate below 20\% and a
confidence score that is measurably anti-correlated with correctness, the
decision of \emph{which} absolute measurements to accept matters more than the
estimator that consumes them. Consensus among temporally adjacent predictions,
evaluated through the motion prior, raises precision from 17.7\% to 99.0\% while
retaining 93\% of correct predictions, and does so without consulting either the
retrieval score or the filter's own belief --- structurally avoiding the
divergence mode in which a drifted filter rejects precisely the measurements
that would correct it.

The limiting factor throughout is availability rather than accuracy. Three
directions follow. First, query-side scale normalisation in the manner of Shao
et al.\ \cite{shao2026altitude}, constructing a single-scale reference database
at the lowest operating altitude and rescaling queries down to it, would remove
both the altitude dependence and the fixed-footprint assumption. Second,
retraining CAEVL at a tile footprint matched to the deployment altitude, rather
than the 14.4$\times$ larger footprint of its current ViLD training data, is a
training run rather than a research problem and is the single change most likely
to move the 35.8\% retrieval figure substantially. Third, resolving the VIO
scale deficit would improve the prior that the consensus test depends upon, and
would reduce the baseline against which the whole system is measured.
